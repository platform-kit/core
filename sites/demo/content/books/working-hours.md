# The first hour

The first hour of a working day is the one you will spend on whatever you look at first. If it is a screen of messages, you will spend it answering; if it is the one piece of work that matters, you will spend it on that. ==The day does not decide what it is about; the first hour does.==

So decide the night before. Not a plan for the whole day — a plan for the first hour, written in one line where you will see it before anything else: the file to open, the paragraph to finish, the call to make. When you sit down there is nothing to choose, and choosing is what the tired part of the morning is worst at.

Keep the hour whole. Close the things that speak to you. If the hour has to start late because of the people you live with, start it late; an hour at nine is worth more than three broken half-hours between seven and ten. What matters is not the clock but the shape: one thing, one stretch, nothing else in it.

You will not manage this every day. Some mornings belong to other people, and that is not a failure of discipline but the price of having a life. The habit is the return, not the streak. ==A first hour kept four days in five is a working life; a first hour kept every day is a fantasy that will make you give up the four.=={sun}

# One thing at a time

There is a kind of tiredness that comes not from working hard but from working on several things at once, none of them finished, all of them open. It is the tiredness of a browser with forty tabs. ==Nothing in you is made for holding six half-done things; you were made to finish one and put it down.==

The remedy is not concentration, which cannot be summoned, but arrangement. Put the one thing in front of you and everything else out of reach — not out of mind, which is impossible, but out of reach, which is only a matter of where things are. The message you cannot see, you cannot answer. The document you have not opened cannot pull at you.

When the one thing is done, or the time for it is done, put it away before you take the next. The putting-away is the whole trick. It is the moment the mind is told the matter is closed, and it is what lets the next thing have all of you.

People fear that working this way will make them slow. The opposite happens. ==Work that is finished stops costing anything; work that is open costs you every minute it stays open, whether or not you are looking at it.==

# The list that ends

A list that can grow forever is not a list; it is a weather system. Most people keep one, and it rains on them all day. ==A useful list has an end, and the end is the point of it.==

Each morning — or the night before, if you keep the first hour — write the list for the day and no further. Five things, six at most. If there are more, they are not for today and the list should say so by leaving them off. A thing left off a list is not lost; it is waiting, and waiting is what most things should be doing most of the time.

When the last item is crossed out, the day's work is done. That is not laziness but accuracy. You said what the day was for, and you did it. Anything you do after that is a gift to tomorrow, and you should feel it as one.

The long list — the one everyone keeps somewhere — can stay. Look at it once a week, not once an hour. Its job is to hold things so you do not have to. ==The short list is for doing; the long one is for forgetting safely.=={sun}

# Stopping on purpose

Most people stop working when they run out — of time, of energy, of ideas. Stopping this way leaves nothing for tomorrow but the memory of running out. ==Stop instead while you still know what comes next, and write it down before you close the door.==

The note is short: the next sentence, the next step, the name of the person to call. It takes a minute, and it buys you the whole of tomorrow's first hour, which would otherwise be spent remembering where you were.

Stopping on purpose also means stopping at a time and not at a feeling. A feeling of being finished is unreliable; it comes when you are tired, not when you are done. A time is honest. When it arrives, you stop, and the work waits, and the waiting does it no harm.

What you do after stopping is not nothing. It is the other half of the work — the walk, the meal, the people, the sleep in which the day's problems get quietly rearranged. ==Rest is not the absence of work; it is where work goes to finish itself.=={sun}
