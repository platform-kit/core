# Say it once

Most writing says its thing three times: once in the introduction, once in the middle, and once in the conclusion, each time a little differently, so that the reader is never sure which version is the real one. ==Say it once, in the place the reader will look, and then stop saying it.==

The place the reader will look is the top. Not the top of the page — the top of the thought. If the thing you have to say is "the meeting is moved to Thursday", then that is the first sentence, and everything after it is detail for those who want detail.

Saying it once is frightening, because it feels thin. Writers pad because a padded thing looks like more work. But the reader is not weighing your work; the reader is trying to find out what you meant. ==A sentence that has been said once and clearly weighs more than the same sentence said three times.==

# The reader is busy

Picture the reader. Not an ideal reader with an afternoon free, but the actual one: on a phone, between two other things, reading with one thumb while someone talks to them. ==Every sentence you write is competing with whatever is happening in the same room as the reader.==

This is not a reason to despair; it is a design constraint, and design constraints are helpful. It tells you the paragraph should be short enough to hold on a phone. It tells you the point should survive being read only to the first line. It tells you the important word should be near the start of the sentence, because that is where the eye lands.

It also tells you what not to do: not to warm up, not to clear your throat, not to explain why you are writing before you write. The reader knows why; they opened it. ==Begin where a busy person would want you to begin, which is at the thing itself.=={sun}

# Cut the second half

Here is a rule that will feel like vandalism the first time and like relief ever after: when you have finished a draft, cut the second half of every paragraph and see whether it is missed. ==Most of the time the first half said it and the second half was you making sure.==

The making-sure is where the padding lives: the restatement, the example that repeats the point, the sentence that begins "in other words". If the reader needed other words they would have asked. Give them the first words, well chosen, and trust them.

Cutting is not the same as being brief for its own sake. A thing that needs four paragraphs should have four. But each of those paragraphs should be the first half of itself. ==Length is not the enemy; repetition is.=={sun}

# Read it aloud

Before you send anything that matters, read it aloud. Not in your head — aloud, at speaking pace, to the empty room. ==The ear catches what the eye forgives.==

You will find the sentence that cannot be said in one breath, which is a sentence that cannot be read in one either. You will find the word you used because it looked impressive and that you would never say to a person. You will find the place where the tone changes, because your voice will change there before you know why.

Fix what you stumbled on and nothing else. Reading aloud is not a chance to rewrite; it is a chance to hear. When you can read the whole thing without stumbling, it is done, and it will be read the way you heard it. ==Writing that can be spoken has already been understood once.=={sun}
