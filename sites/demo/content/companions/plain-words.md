# Say it once

## Summary

Most writing says its point three times, so the reader never knows which version is real. Say it once, at the top of the thought, and let everything after it be detail. Saying it once feels thin, and writers pad because padding looks like work — but the reader is finding out what you meant, not weighing your effort.

## Essence

- Say it once, where the reader will look.
- The top of the thought, not the top of the page.
- The reader is not weighing your work.

# The reader is busy

## Summary

Picture the real reader: on a phone, between two things, reading with one thumb. Every sentence competes with the room. This is a design constraint, not a despair: paragraphs short enough for a phone, points that survive the first line, the important word near the start. And no warming up — begin at the thing itself.

## Essence

- Every sentence competes with the reader's room.
- A constraint is a design tool.
- Begin at the thing itself.

# Cut the second half

## Summary

Cut the second half of every paragraph and see whether it is missed; usually the first half said it and the second was you making sure. That is where the padding lives — the restatement, the repeated example, "in other words". Cutting is not brevity for its own sake: a thing that needs four paragraphs should have four, each the first half of itself.

## Essence

- The second half is usually you making sure.
- Give the first words, well chosen, and trust the reader.
- Length is not the enemy; repetition is.

# Read it aloud

## Summary

Before sending anything that matters, read it aloud at speaking pace. The ear catches what the eye forgives: the sentence too long for one breath, the word you would never say to a person, the place where the tone changes. Fix only what you stumbled on; when you can read it through, it is done.

## Essence

- The ear catches what the eye forgives.
- Fix what you stumbled on and nothing else.
- Writing that can be spoken has been understood once.
